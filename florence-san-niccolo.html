Getting the taxi

Two adults and two children fit a standard Florence taxi. With four large cases, ask for a maxi.

At the station today

  • Out of the main exit onto Piazza della Stazione. The rank is immediately left, marked TAXI. Join the queue.
  • Licensed taxis are white with a roof sign and a door number. Don't take a ride from anyone who approaches you inside the station.
  • Say the address: Via di San Niccolò quarantadue. The street is inside the restricted traffic zone but taxis are exempt, so you'll get to the door.
  • Card or cash. No tip expected — round up a euro or two.

Booking one later in the week

  • You can't flag one down in Florence. Ranks, phone or app only.
  • Phone 055 4390 or 055 4242. They give you a car name and number — something like Milano 23 — and an arrival time, usually five to ten minutes.
  • Apps: appTaxi and IT Taxi are the Italian ones. Free Now also works. Uber is black-car only here, roughly double.
  • The meter starts when the car is dispatched, so a booked taxi always reads higher than one from a rank.

Where you are

San Niccolò is a single long street on the south bank, pinned between the river and the hill. It runs east from Via dei Bardi to the medieval gate at Piazza Poggi, and that's essentially the whole neighbourhood — a few hundred metres of five-storey stone houses with the green slope of Monte alle Croci rising directly behind them.

It is the quietest central address in Florence. Tour groups don't come here, because there's nothing on the street they've heard of. What there is instead: eight or nine restaurants, two wine bars, a pub, a gelateria, a handful of artisan workshops, and a steady trickle of people walking through on their way up to the viewpoint.

You are on the edge of everything without being in it. Cross Ponte alle Grazie and you're at Santa Croce in five minutes. Walk west along the river and you reach Ponte Vecchio in ten, the Uffizi in twelve, Santo Spirito in fifteen. Walk the other way, through the gate, and you're climbing into gardens.

On the street itself

Working from the western end: Osteria di Rocco and the little park on Via dei Renai; Rifrullo on the corner, open from breakfast to the small hours; Clet's studio tucked down Via dell'Olmo; the gelateria at the foot of Via San Miniato; three restaurants in a row; the Joshua Tree; then the street narrows and runs out at the gate.

Practicalities

  • Food shopping. A small alimentari on the street covers basics. For anything real, the Mercato di Sant'Ambrogio is fifteen minutes over the bridge, 7.00–14.00 daily except Sunday.
  • Water. Public fountains at Piazza Poggi and in the rose garden. Tap water is fine.
  • Buses. 12 and 13 run along Lungarno Serristori up to Piazzale Michelangelo. Contactless, tap on board.
  • Bikes. RideMovi electric bikes are scattered around; scan the QR on the handlebar. No use uphill, good along the river.
  • Evenings. Residential and quiet by midnight. The noisy bars are over in Santa Croce — close enough to walk to, far enough not to hear.

The walk west

If you only do one aimless walk this week, do this one: out of the door, west along Via di San Niccolò, into Via dei Bardi where the street curves under the hill, past the flood plaques, and out at Ponte Vecchio. Ten minutes, passes almost nothing famous, and is one of the loveliest stretches in the city.

The gate that was never cut down

The tower at the end of your street was built around 1324, part of Florence's sixth and last ring of walls. It is the only one of the city's gates still standing at full height.

All the others were lopped off in the mid-1500s. Artillery had arrived, and a tall stone tower had gone from being a defensive advantage to a large obvious target. Porta San Niccolò was spared because it sits tight against the hill — anyone attacking would have been firing downwards from above, so height made no difference. It survived by being in an awkward spot.

Michelangelo's mattresses

In 1529 the emperor's army laid siege to Florence, and the city put Michelangelo — a sculptor with no military training — in charge of the fortifications. He fortified San Miniato at the top of your hill, because whoever held that hill held the city.

The bell tower was the problem: it drew cannon fire. His solution was to hang mattresses stuffed with wool down the sides, so the shot struck something soft and lost its force. It worked. The tower is still there.

Poggi's ramps

In 1865 Florence became capital of the new Kingdom of Italy and set about remaking itself for the part. An architect named Giuseppe Poggi got the job. On the north bank he demolished the medieval walls entirely and replaced them with boulevards. On this side the walls survived — you can still trace them climbing the hill — and he built something else instead.

The Rampe del Poggi, finished in the 1870s, are the terraced ramps from the gate up to the viewpoint: switchbacks, retaining walls, fountains and artificial grottoes built from rough stone and shells. They fell derelict for decades and were restored and reopened in 2019. Piazzale Michelangelo at the top is his too, complete with the bronze David that took eighteen oxen to drag up there in 1873.

Florence was the capital for six years. The government moved to Rome in 1871 and left the city bankrupt from the building works.

4 November 1966

After days of rain the Arno broke its banks before dawn and put central Florence under water. San Niccolò, low and hard against the river, was among the worst hit — the water here rose over four metres, higher than the ground-floor ceilings.

Thirty-odd people died and millions of books and artworks were ruined. The volunteers who came from all over the world to dig them out were called the angeli del fango, the mud angels.

Look for the small marble plaques set into walls along your street and around Santa Croce, each with a line and the date, marking where the water reached. Some are well above head height.

Before all that

This was a working district, not a grand one. The Arno powered the wool trade, and the riverside quarters were full of tanners, dyers and fullers — trades that stank and were pushed to the edge of town. The grand palaces are all on the other bank. What you have here are tall narrow houses built for people who worked with their hands, which is why the street still feels like a street rather than a monument.

Up the hill, the Forte di Belvedere was built for Grand Duke Ferdinando I in 1590. Officially it defended the city. In practice it pointed inwards as much as outwards, and gave the Medici somewhere to retreat to if the Florentines ever decided they'd had enough of them.

The street-sign artist lives two minutes away

Clet Abrahams is a French artist who has spent twenty years adding removable stickers to Florence's road signs. A no-entry sign gets a little man carrying the white bar away. A one-way arrow becomes Cupid's arrow through a heart. The city has never quite decided whether to be annoyed.

CLET Studio

Via dell'Olmo 8r · 2 min from the door · closed Tuesdays · stickers from €1

His actual workshop, on a lane off your street. Stickers, prints, T-shirts, playing cards. Buy a sheet and then spend the week spotting his signs around the city — once you start you can't stop, and there are dozens in the centre.

Paper and scent

Il Papiro

Several branches, including Via dei Tavolini 13r · daily 10.30–19.00

Florentine marbled paper, made by floating pigment on water and laying a sheet on top. Notebooks, wax seals, dip pens, ink they mix themselves. Catch them working and they'll usually demonstrate the marbling.

A pharmacy run by Dominican monks since 1221, still in its frescoed halls. Buy nothing — go for the rooms and the smell, which is unlike any shop you've been in.

The gelato project

Give it the week and rank them properly. Two tells for the real thing: it should sit in covered steel tins, not piled in bright mounds under glass, and the pistachio should be a dull khaki. Bright green means dyed.

Start with Il Gelato di Filo at the bottom of your street, then Gelateria dei Neri over the bridge, then Cantina del Gelato on Via de' Bardi. Three data points is enough to start an argument.

The dome nobody could build

The cathedral was started in 1296 with a hole at the top, because the Florentines designed a dome bigger than anyone knew how to build and decided to work it out later. The hole stayed open for over a century.

The problem: a dome that size normally needs a wooden framework underneath to hold it up while it's built, and no forest in Tuscany had trees long enough. Brunelleschi won the commission in 1418 by claiming he could do it with no framework at all — the dome would hold itself up as it went.

He managed it with two shells, one inside the other, and four million bricks laid in a herringbone pattern that locks each course into the one below so nothing slides off before the mortar sets. He also had to invent the machines: a reversible ox-driven hoist to lift stone hundreds of feet, and a crane that could swing a load sideways once it got up there.

Six hundred years on there's still argument about exactly how. A Florentine architect called Massimo Ricci spent his career on the question and built a half-million-brick scale replica in a park east of the city to prove his theory.

Your own gate

Porta San Niccolò, 1324, at the end of the street — the only city gate in Florence still at its original full height. The rest were cut down in the 1500s because cannon had made tall towers a liability. Yours survived because it sits under the hill, where an attacker would be shooting downwards anyway, so height didn't matter. In summer you can sometimes climb the internal stair.

Mattresses against cannonballs

During the 1529 siege, Michelangelo was put in charge of defending Florence. He fortified San Miniato at the top of your hill and protected its bell tower from artillery by hanging wool-stuffed mattresses down the sides so the shot hit something soft. It held.

The bridge that didn't get blown up

Ponte Vecchio was lined with butchers until 1593, when Grand Duke Ferdinando I evicted them — the smell was reaching his private corridor overhead — and let only goldsmiths trade there. They're still the only shops on it.

In August 1944 the retreating German army destroyed every bridge in Florence. Ponte Vecchio was the single exception; instead they demolished the medieval buildings at each end to block the approaches. Nobody is certain why it was spared.

The corridor overhead is the Vasari Corridor, built in 1565 so the Medici could walk from the town hall to their palace without setting foot among the public. It runs almost a kilometre, straight through buildings and over the bridge.

Galileo's finger

Museo Galileo

Piazza dei Giudici 1 · 12 min walk · ~€14, reduced under 18

Galileo's two surviving telescopes, the lens he used to find Jupiter's moons, and several centuries of beautiful brass instruments for measuring things. Also, in a glass egg on a stand, his actual middle finger — removed when he was reburied in 1737 and displayed ever since, pointing upward. Nobody thinks this is normal and everyone goes to look.

A Roman arena under the streets

Walk through Piazza de' Peruzzi near Santa Croce and look at the buildings on the north side: they curve in a smooth arc for no visible reason. They're standing on the foundations of a second-century Roman amphitheatre, 120 metres across, that held twenty thousand people. It's never been excavated. It's just still down there, shaping the street plan seventeen centuries later.

The high-water marks

On 4 November 1966 the Arno flooded and the water in this neighbourhood rose over four metres. Small marble plaques are set into walls around here and by Santa Croce, each with a line and the date, marking where it reached. Find one and stand next to it.
